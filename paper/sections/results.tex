\section{Results}
\label{results}

We compared the performance of the proposed methods by estimating the two structural 
parameters of interest $\tau_F$ and $\tau_C$ using the weighted least squares. 
For FE-MSM, the parameters are estimated by solving the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:least_squares}
(\widehat{\tau}_F,\widehat{\tau}_C)
=
\arg\min_{\tau_F',\tau_C'}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\widehat{W}_i
\left\{
Y_i-\alpha-\tau_F' A_{iT}
-\tau_C' \sum_{t=T-3}^{T-1} A_{it}
\right\}^{2}
\end{equation}
Where $\widehat{W}_i$ are the stabilized version of 
IPTW weights defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:iptw_weights}. 
The parametric 
propensity score model is correctly specified (up to a unit-specific fixed effect) 
as a logistic regression of the 
treatment on the past treatment history and covariates, with a unit-specific fixed effect, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ps_model}
p(a_{it} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_{it}, a_{i,t-1}, \alpha_i) = \operatorname{expit}(w_1^\top \mathbf{x}_{it} + w_2 a_{i,t-1} + \alpha_i).
\end{equation}


For the sequential GPLVM, a $\text{CMM}(1)$ with a one-dimensional latent variable 
(i.e., $\mathbf{Z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^J$ with $J = 1$) 
is fitted to the treatment data, 
using a linear kernel: 
\[
k_t^{\text{treat}} ((\mathbf{x}_t,a_{t-1},\mathbf{z}), (\mathbf{x}'_t,a'_{t-1},\mathbf{z}'))= \nu \left[\sum_{j=1}^{d_X}x_{jt} x'_{jt} 
+ a_{t-1}a'_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{J} z_{j} z'_{j} \right]
\]
where $\nu$ is the variance hyperparameter. This is equivalent to 
the Bayesian linear regression with a latent variable. The observed confounders 
are standardized before fitting the GPLVM. A  
weakly informative Gamma prior is placed on 
$\nu$, specifically $\nu \sim \Gamma(2,1)$.
The parameters of the GPLVM are estimated by minimizing the negative evidence 
lower bound using the Adam optimizer with learning rate set to 
$0.01$. The number of iterations is set to $6000$ and the convergence is 
monitored by checking the change in ELBO.
The parameters of the MSM are estimated by
the procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:seqgplvm-outcome} by solving 
the same optimization problem as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:least_squares} with 
number of posterior draws set to $S=100$. The following logistic regression 
model is used to estimate the propensity scores for the IPTW estimation 
of the MSM parameters:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ps_model_seqgplvm}
p(a_{it} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_{it}, a_{i,t-1}, \mathbf{z}_i^{(s)}) = 
\operatorname{expit}\!\left(w_3^\top \mathbf{x}_{it} + w_4 a_{i,t-1}+ w_5^\top \mathbf{z}_i^{(s)}+ w_6 \right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_i^{(s)}$ is the $s$-th posterior draw of the latent variable for individual $i$.

For both methods, the weighting is stabilized by multiplying the weights with 
conditional probabilities of the current treatment given only the previous treatment using 
the following logistic regression model:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ps_model_stabilized}
p(a_{it} = 1 \mid a_{i,t-1}) = \operatorname{expit}\!\left(w_7 a_{i,t-1} + w_8\right).
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Trimming weights.} The unit-specific fixed effects and the individual 
latent variables are not identified 
for individuals with no variation in their treatment history, i.e., always 
treated or never treated. Including these samples in the estimation process can make 
the optimizations 
for both methods unstable. Even if the population level positivity assumption holds, 
in the finite sample, it is 
possible to have such individuals. This problem becomes more severe when the 
number of time points is small. 
In this case, we removed always and never treated 
individuals from the estimation process and imputed their propensity 
scores by assigning a fixed value, e.g., $\hat{\pi}_{it} = 0.01$ for never treated 
individuals and $\hat{\pi}_{it} = 0.99$ for always treated individuals for all-time points. Depending on the length of the treatment intervention window $k$ specified 
in the MSM, this imputation strategy can lead to extreme weights which 
we further trimmed by capping them within the range of $[10^{-6}, 1-10^{-6}]$ 
to avoid unstable weights. 

Figures~\ref{fig:simulation_bias_combined_a_p2}--\ref{fig:simulation_coverage_combined_a_p2} show the results of the simulation study for the 
two covariate-case. The additional results for the four covariate-case are 
reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:additional_simulation_results}. Bias, standard error, and coverage of the 
90\% confidence intervals are reported based on 500 simulation runs. 
Figure~\ref{fig:simulation_bias_combined_a_p2} reports the bias, 
Figure~\ref{fig:simulation_standard_error_combined_a_p2} reports the standard error, and 
Figure~\ref{fig:simulation_coverage_combined_a_p2} reports the coverage. 
Within each figure, the first two rows correspond to the weak unmeasured confounding setting where 
$U_i \in [-1,1]$ ($c_U=1$), and the last two rows correspond to the strong unmeasured confounding 
setting where $U_i \in [-2,2]$ ($c_U=2$). The first and third rows correspond to 
$\tau_F = 1$, while the second and fourth rows correspond to $\tau_C = 0.3$. 
The columns correspond to the $N$ to $T$ ratio, with panels (A), (D), (G), and (J) showing 
$\rho = 5$ (the largest number of time points), panels (B), (E), (H), and (K) showing 
$\rho = 10$, and panels (C), (F), (I), and (L) showing $\rho = 50$ 
(the smallest number of time points). Solid lines in grey show the results using the 
true propensity scores (\texttt{IPTW-True}). Solid orange lines 
show the results for FE-MSM (\texttt{FE-MSM}) and dash-dotted 
purple lines show the results for SeqGPLVM (\texttt{SeqGPLVM-MSM}). 
Dashed blue lines 
represent the results for the naive IPTW estimaton of the specified MSM assuming 
sequential ignorability without adjusting for the unmeasured confounder (\texttt{IPTW}). 
In this case we used the same logistic regression as 
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ps_model} to estimate the propensity scores by replacing 
the individual 
fixed effects $\alpha_i$ with a global intercept.

The results show that both methods improves the bias of the estimates of the 
parameters of interest in comparison to the naive IPTW, especially as the ratio of 
$\frac{N}{T}$ decreases. However, FE-MSM tends to have slightly lower bias and 
standard error compared to the SeqGPLVM-MSM, particularly for smaller sample sizes. 
This may be due to the fact that the FE-MSM method relies on a correctly specified 
parametric model for the propensity scores, while the sequential GPLVM method is more 
flexible but may require larger sample sizes to achieve similar performance. We can see that 
the worst case for both methods is for $\rho = 50$, but specially this is visible for 
the sequential GPLVM in the smallest sample size $N = 200$, where it introduces 
more bias than the naive IPTW for the strong unmeasured confounding case 
(Figure~\ref{fig:simulation_bias_combined_a_p2}, panels (I) and (L)) 
and performs nearly as poorly as the naive IPTW for the weak unmeasured confounding 
case (Figure~\ref{fig:simulation_bias_combined_a_p2}, panels (C) and (F)). However, 
the larger estimated standard errors in this setting do not generally keep the coverage 
close to the nominal level. In particular, for the shortest treatment sequence 
($\rho = 50$) under the stronger unmeasured confounding setting 
(Figure~\ref{fig:simulation_coverage_combined_a_p2}, panels (I) and (L)), the coverage of 
\texttt{SeqGPLVM-MSM} falls well below $0.90$, 
especially in panel (L), corresponding to the $\tau_C = 0.3$ scenario. Thus, 
in the worst-case regime, the increase in uncertainty is not sufficient to 
compensate for the additional bias.

As the number of time points increases, the gap between the bias of two methods decreases, and 
they perform more similarly although the FE-MSM bias remains lower while the standard 
error of SeqGPLVM-MSM remains higher than the FE-MSM estimates across all scenarios. 
Under the weak unmeasured confounding setting where the unmeasured confounder explains 
roughly 1/3 of the variance of the treatment assignments (Figure~\ref{fig:simulation_coverage_combined_a_p2}, panels (A)--(F)), coverage is mixed 
rather than uniformly close to the nominal level. For $\tau_C = 0.3$, \texttt{FE-MSM} 
attains coverage values below $0.90$ throughout, and \texttt{SeqGPLVM-MSM} 
shows substantial undercoverage for the shortest treatment sequence, 
with the deterioration being most pronounced for $N = 500$ and $N = 1000$ (panel (F)). 
For $\tau_F = 1.0$, both methods are generally closer to the nominal level, 
although some undercoverage remains, again most visibly for $\rho = 50$ (panel (C)). 
In this weaker confounding setting, \texttt{FE-MSM} is generally 
closer to \texttt{IPTW-True} than \texttt{SeqGPLVM-MSM}, 
but it does not match the true-propensity benchmark, especially for the 
shortest treatment sequence. Even when $c_U = 1$, \texttt{FE-MSM} 
still exhibits noticeably larger bias and lower coverage than \texttt{IPTW-True}, 
and this gap becomes more pronounced under stronger unmeasured confounding. 

In the stronger unmeasured confoundedness case where the unmeasured confounder explains 
2/3 of the variance in the treatment (Figures~\ref{fig:simulation_bias_combined_a_p2}--\ref{fig:simulation_coverage_combined_a_p2}, panels (G)--(L)), both methods perform more poorly 
than the weaker case in terms of the bias while FE-MSM performs slightly better across all different $\rho$ values and 
sample sizes. In this scenario there is a small Monte Carlo bias observed even for the 
\texttt{IPTW-True}, due to extreme weights and the truncation strategy.
