\section{SeqGPLVM-MSM}
\label{sec:seqgplvm}

SeqGPLVM in combination with MSM consists of three
stages: (i) a latent variable model (SeqGPLVM) that estimates a substitute for the
unmeasured confounder based on the observed treatment sequence, (ii) estimating
the propensity scores using a treatment model that conditions on both observed
covariates and the confounder substitute, and (iii) a marginal
structural model that uses the estimated propensity scores in stage two to adjust
for confounding using IPTW. It is worth mentioning that SeqGPLM can be used
in conjunction with any other estimation methods as well, such as g-computation or doubly
robust methods, but we focus on IPTW estimation of MSM for comparability with
FE-MSM.

\subsection{Sequential deconfounding}

The sequential deconfounding theory proposed by 
\citeA{hattSequentialDeconfoundingCausal2024} can be seen as the sequential version of 
the multi-treatment deconfounder proposed by \citeA{wangBlessingsMultipleCauses2019}. Similarly 
to the multi-treatment deconfounder, it is based on the three key assumptions that are 
described below.

%is a probabilistic 
%longitudinal latent variable 
%model. 

\begin{idassumpSG}[CMM(p): Conditional Markov Model of order p]\label{ass:SG-Marcovianity}
    A latent variable $\mathbf{Z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^J $, a fixed order $p \in \mathbb{N}^*$
    and a parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\gamma} = (\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\gamma}_T)$ exist such that the assigned treatment distribution can be 
    factorized as: 
    \begin{equation}
    p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{iT} \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{iT}, \mathbf{z}_i) 
    = \prod_{t=1}^T p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_t}(a_{it} \mid \mathbf{a}_{i,t-p:t-1}, \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{\leq t}, \mathbf{z}_i). 
    \label{eq:cmm}
    \end{equation}
    for $t<1$, $\mathbf{a}_t$ is set to $0$ by convention. We then say that the 
    latent variable $\mathbf{Z}_i$ follows a Conditional Markov Model of order $p$, 
    written $\mathbf{Z}_i \sim \text{CMM} (p)$\footnote{The original version of this assumption 
in \citeA{hattSequentialDeconfoundingCausal2024} is stated only for a first-order CMM. However, 
the proof of their Theorem 1 using Preposition 7.6 in \citeA{kallenbergFoundationsModernProbability}, 
can be easily extended to higher-order CMMs as well 
(see \href{https://chatgpt.com/share/69c7c2fe-2f64-8388-8843-b0d989e7b267}{link} 
and \cite{bouchattaouiCausalDynamicVariational2025}). Hence the more general 
form of the CMM assumption is stated.}.
\end{idassumpSG}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:cmm},
\(\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\gamma}_T\}\) are the generative 
parameters of the treatment assignment model. Note that the conditional 
probability 
distribution at
each time point can have different parameters. 
This is crucial to understand the SeqGPLVM architecture. 

\begin{idassumpSG}[No unmeasured single-time-point confounding]
    \label{ass:SG-No-single-time-point-confounding}
    All confounders that only affect at a single time point are observed. 
    Equivalently, any unobserved confounder influencing $A_t$ must also induce 
    dependence with at least one other treatment $A_s$ ($s\neq t$), conditional on the observed history.
\end{idassumpSG}
This assumption excludes structures such as the one in 
Figure~\ref{fig:single-time-point-confounding}. Note that, Assumption~\ref{ass:C-timeinvariant-uc} 
is not 
sufficient to exclude these single-time-point confounders since a confounder can 
be constant but its effect 
varry with time.

\begin{idassumpSG}[Consistency of the substitute confounder]
    \label{ass:SG-Consistency}
The sequential deconfounder assumes that all latent variables $\mathbf{Z}_i\sim\text{CMM}(p)$ for a particular 
$p$ are pinpointed 
by a single deterministic function of treatment and observed covariates history. 
Formally, there exists a measurable function
$f$ such that
\[
p(\mathbf{z}_i \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{iT}, \bar{\mathbf{a}}_{iT}) = 
\delta_{f(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{iT},\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{iT})}.
\]    
\end{idassumpSG}
Assumption~\ref{ass:SG-Consistency} implies the uniqueness of the latent variable $\mathbf{Z}_i$ which 
imposes the $\text{CMM}$ of order $p$. 
 This assumption is necessary since only the existance of a $\text{CMM}(p)$, does not guarantee the uniqueness of the latent 
 variable. The violation of this assumption can lead to 
 contradictory causal estimates \cite{damourMultiCauseApproachesCausal2019}. 
 However, the substitute confounder $\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_i$ is not 
 required to coincide with the true confounder $\mathbf{U}_i$ or the true latent factorizer 
 $\mathbf{Z}_i$. It is sufficient 
 to estimate the substitue confounder up to some deterministic bijective 
 transformation, e.g., a linear 
 transformation.  

Under these assumptions
and the common assumptions, Theorem 1 in \citeA{hattSequentialDeconfoundingCausal2024} 
states that we obtain sequential ignorability 
conditioned on the observed covariates' history, treatment history and the 
latent variables $\mathbf{Z}_i$. For all $t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}$ and for all 
$\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{it}$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:seqgplvm-single-unit-seqignorability}
    Y_{i}(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{T}) \perp A_t \mid \bar{\mathbf{X}}_{it}, \bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,t-1} ,\mathbf{Z}_{i}.
\end{equation}
This theorem makes a bridge between the observational world and the potential outcomes world. 
More importantly, this theorem does not restrict the class of models that can be used to 
estimate the substitute confounder. It holds for any 
latent variable model that can estimate a latent variable $\mathbf{Z}_i$ in a CMM of chosen order $p$. 
The key idea of the deconfounder is that 
once the substitute confounder is estimated, we can 
treat it as a \emph{observed-like} covariates and include it in 
any outcome model—depending on the specific causal target—to adjust for 
confounding. 

\subsection{SeqGPLVM}
Sequential Gaussian process latent variable model (SeqGPLVM) 
is a specific implementation of the sequential deconfounding theory based 
on the Gaussian process latent variable model (GPLVM)
\cite{lawrenceGaussianProcessLatent2003, titsiasBayesianGaussianProcess2010}. 
GPLVM is a non-parametric probabilistic latent variable model that can be 
used to estimate a low-dimensional 
latent representation of high-dimensional data. SeqGPLVM extends 
GPLVM to the sequential setting by modeling the treatment assignment 
mechanism as a CMM using Gaussian processes. 

%It leverages the 
%flexibility of Gaussian processes to capture complex, non-linear 
%relationships between the latent confounder substitutes, observed 
%covariates, and treatment assignments over time.

The objective of SeqGPLVM is to estimate the posterior distribution of 
the latent variable $\mathbf{Z}_i$ with a chosen dimension $J$, given the observed treatment history and covariates,
$p(\mathbf{z}_i \mid \bar{\mathbf{a}}_{iT}, \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{iT})$, while imposing the CMM structure on the treatment
assignment mechanism. To estimate this posterior 
using the 
Bayesian framework, a prior and 
a likelihood model are specified. We assign the following prior to the 
latent variables assuming they are i.i.d. across individuals and the latent dimensions:
\[
p(\mathbf{z}) = \prod_{i=1}^N p(\mathbf{z}_i),
\qquad \text{where} \qquad
\mathbf{Z}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_J)
\quad \text{for } i=1,\ldots,N.
\]

The SeqGPLVM defines a generative mapping, $f_t$, from the joint space of the latent 
confounder substitutes, 
observed previous treatment history and covariates, to the treatment assignment 
at each time point: 
\[
f_t : \mathcal{Z} \times \mathcal{X}_{1} \times \cdots \times \mathcal{X}_{\leq t} \times \mathcal{A}_{t-p} \times \cdots \times \mathcal{A}_{t-1} \to \mathcal{A}_t.
\]
We model these mappings as non-parametric functions using Gaussian processes (GPs), 
i.e., \(f_t \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k_t(\cdot, \cdot))\) for \(t = 1, \ldots, T\), 
where \(k_t\) is the kernel function at time \(t\). 
Since the kernels are defined on the joint space of the latent substitutes 
and the observed history, 
there is a lot of flexibility to encode different types of interactions between 
these variables. For the sake 
of simplicity, we use the same kernel for all time points, but in principle, 
we can use different kernels at different time points. The generative model then 
can be written as follows: 

\[
p(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{iT}, \bar{\mathbf{f}}_T  \mid \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{iT}, \mathbf{z}_i) = 
\prod_{t=1}^T p(a_{it} 
\mid f_t)p(f_t \mid \mathbf{z}_i, \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i,\leq t}, \bar{\mathbf{a}}_{i,t-p:t-1})).
\]

Since the exact posterior distribution is intractable, we adopt the 
variational inference with inducing point for inference \cite{titsiasBayesianGaussianProcess2010}. 
The dimensionality of the inducing points is \(|\mathcal{Z}| + \sum_{s=1}^{\leq t} |\mathcal{X}_s| + \sum_{s=t-p}^{t-1}|\mathcal{A}_{s}|\). 
The likelihood term \(p(a_{it} \mid f_t)\) depends on the type of treatment variable. 
For instance if \(\mathcal{A_t}\in \{0,1\}\), we can use a Bernoulli likelihood, 
or for a continuous treatment, we can use a Gaussian likelihood. The details of the 
variational inference procedure and the optimization of the evidence lower bound (ELBO) are provided 
in Appendix~\ref{sec:seqgplvm_loss}. In Fig \ref{fig:seqgplvm_architecture} a 
graphical representation of the SeqGPLVM is shown.

\subsection{Outcome model}
\label{sec:seqgplvm-outcome}
Once the approximate posterior distribution of the substitute confounder is estimated, 
it can be used to adjust for confounding in the outcome model. This stage 
closely parallels the FE-MSM procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:fe-msm}. In
particular, Theorem~1 implies identification of the mean truncated potential outcome
over the last $k$ time periods through the same IPTW representation as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:truncated_potential_outcome_id}, with the unobserved fixed effect
$\alpha_i$ replaced by the latent variable $\boldsymbol{Z}_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:truncated_potential_outcome_id_seqgplvm}
    \mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_i(\underline{\mathbf{a}}_{T-k})\bigr]
    =
    \mathbb{E}\left[
    \frac{\mathbf{1}\bigl(\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{i,T-k} = \underline{\mathbf{a}}_{T-k}\bigr)\, Y_i}
    {\prod_{t=T-k}^{T} \pi_{it}^{a_t} (1 - \pi_{it})^{1-a_t}}
    \right],
\end{equation}
where
\[
\pi_{it}
=
\mathbb{P}(A_{it}=1 \mid \bar{\mathbf{X}}_{it}, \bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,t-1}, \boldsymbol{Z}_i).
\]
Hence, as in FE-MSM, estimation proceeds in two steps: first, a parametric model is used
to estimate the propensity scores, and second, these estimated propensities are used to
construct inverse-probability weights for fitting the marginal structural model. The key
difference is that instead of directly estimating a unit-specific fixed effect $\alpha_i$ 
in the treatment model, a sample is drawn from the approximate posterior distribution 
of the substitute confounder $q(\boldsymbol{Z}_i)$ and 
condition the treatment model on these samples together with the observed history 
to estimate the propensity scores.

To account for uncertainty in the inferred substitute confounder, we adopt a
multiple-imputation-style procedure. Specifically, for each posterior draw 
$\mathbf{z}_i^{(s)} \sim q(\boldsymbol{Z}_i)$, $s=1,\dots,S$, the propensity scores are 
re-estimated and the corresponding IPTW weights are computed as,
\[
\widehat{W}_i(\hat\beta, \mathbf{z}_i^{(s)})
=
\prod_{t=T-k}^{T}
\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{it}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\mathbf{z}_i^{(s)})}\right)^{A_{it}}
\left(\frac{1}{1-\pi_{it}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\mathbf{z}_i^{(s)})}\right)^{1-A_{it}},
\]
and fit the same marginal structural model as in the FE-MSM approach. Let
\[
\Psi_N(\boldsymbol{\theta}_s)
:=
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
\widehat{W}_i(\hat\beta, \mathbf{z}_i^{(s)})\,
\mathbf{h}\!\left(\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{i,T-k}\right)\,
\left[
Y_i - m\!\left(\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{i,T-k}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_s \right)
\right].
\]
For each draw $s$, the corresponding MSM parameter estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)}$ is
obtained by solving
\[
\Psi_N(\boldsymbol{\theta}_s)=0.
\]
Equivalently, when the MSM is linear and $\mathbf{h}(\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{i,T-k})$ is chosen
appropriately, this step can be implemented as a weighted least squares regression using
the draw-specific IPTW weights.

The final point estimate is then obtained by averaging across the $S$ posterior draws,
\[
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)}.
\]

This procedure introduces two sources of uncertainty: first, uncertainty about the
latent substitute confounder induced by the approximate posterior $q(\boldsymbol{Z}_i)$, and second,
the usual sampling uncertainty in the MSM fit conditional on a given latent draw. To
propagate both sources into the final causal effect estimates, we combine the
draw-specific estimates using Rubin's within-between decomposition \cite{rubinMultipleImputationNonresponse2009}. 
Let
$V_{j}^{(s)}$ denote the robust (sandwich) variance 
estimate associated with the $j$-th component of the 
MSM parameter vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)}$ for draw $s$, 
and let $Q_j$ represent the average of these variances across 
draws for this component, 
then the total variance estimator for 
the $j$-th component of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is given by

\[
Q_j = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} V_{j}^{(s)},
\qquad
B_j = \frac{1}{S - 1}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_j^{(s)} - \bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_j\right)^2,
\qquad
T_j = Q_j + \left(1+\frac{1}{S}\right)B_j.
\]
The resulting confidence interval $T_j$ therefore reflect both
(i) variability in the MSM fit conditional on a latent draw and
(ii) additional variability due to uncertainty in the inferred substitute confounder.
