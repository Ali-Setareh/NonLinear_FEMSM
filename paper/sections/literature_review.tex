\chapter{Background and related work}
\label{chap:literature_review}

Methods for observational longitudinal causal inference can be organized along two main dimensions: 
the structure of the causal diagram and the causal target of interest. 
The structure of the causal setting is typically represented by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) 
that encodes the assumptions about the data generating process and the causal relationships 
between the variables \cite{pearlCausalityModelsReasoning2009}. The presence or absence of 
treatment-confounder feedback 
and unobserved confounders are important features of the causal structure that can affect the 
choice of estimation method. The causal target of interest, on the other hand, is driven by the 
research question and scientific context of the study \cite{lundbergWhatYourEstimand2021}. Different methods may be designed to estimate 
different causal targets, such as the average treatment effect at a specific time point, the cumulative 
effect of treatment over time, or the average contrast between two specific treatment regime. Having these 
two dimensions in mind, in the 
following sections, we will review the relevant literature and concepts on longitudinal causal inference 
and the methods exist to address confounding.

\section{Conceptual foundations of longitudinal causal inference.}
\paragraph{Potential outcomes and treatment regimes}
The causal targets are often formalized in the potential-outcomes framework \cite{rubinEstimatingCausalEffects1974}. 
In this framework, the potential outcome of individual $i$ is defined as $Y_i(a)$, representing the outcome that would be observed if 
the treatment $A$ were set to a specific value $a$. Note the difference between the observed outcome $Y_i$ and the 
potential outcome $Y_i(a)$ which is an unobserved variable, 
as we can only observe the outcome under the treatment that was actually assigned to. Similarly, in the longitudinal setting, we can define the potential outcome at time $t$ for a 
static treatment regime\footnote{The static here is to contrast with a 
dynamic treatment regime, where treatments depends on the values of the confounders \cite{moodieDemystifyingOptimalDynamic2007}. In this thesis we only consider static treatment regimes.} 
$\bar{a}_t = (a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_t)$ as $Y_{i,t+1}(\bar{a}_t)$, 
representing the outcome that would be observed if the treatment were set to specific values at each 
time point. In single terminal outcome setting, it is common to drop the time index on the potential outcome and simply write $Y_i(\bar{a}_T)$ to represent the potential outcome at the end of the study under treatment regime $\bar{a}_T$. 
In a similar way, the 
potential value of confounders at time $t$ can also be defined as $X_{i,t}(\bar{a}_{t-1})$, 
representing the value of the confounder at time $t$ that would be observed if the treatment 
were set to specific values at each time point up to $t-1$\footnote{Here we follow the Robins indexing convention to emphasize the temporal ordering of the treatments, 
confounders and (repeated) outcome \cite{hernanCausalInferenceWhat2023}. Counfounders at time time $t$ is measured after the treatment at time $t-1$ 
and before the treatment at time $t$. Moreover, the potential outcome is indexed at time $t+1$ to reflect 
the fact that the outcome is determined after the treatment while the measured outcome at time $t$ may act 
as a confounder for the treatment at time $t$ and the outcomes at later time points.}.

\paragraph{Treatment-confounder feedback.}
Treatment-confounder feedback occurs when the treatment at time $t$ can affect the confounders at later 
time points. This creates a feedback loop between the treatment and the confounders, 
which can lead to bias if not properly adjusted for; see \cite{hernanCausalInferenceWhat2023booksection} 
for detailed examples. In Figure \ref{fig:sample_dags} (b), these feedback loops 
are represented by the purple arrows from $A_t$ to subsequent $X_{t+k}$ where $1 \le k \le T-t$. These structures 
are problematic for standard regression-based methods which estimate 
models such as $\mathbb{E}[Y_i|\bar{A}_3,\bar{X}_3] = \alpha + \sum_{t=1}^{3} \beta_t A_t + \sum_{t=1}^{3}\gamma_t X_t$ 
for a terminal outcome and three 
time points, even 
if this outcome regression is correctly specified. To see why, in Figure \ref{fig:sample_dags} (b), 
$X_3$ is a confounder for the effect of $A_3$ on $Y$ as it is a common cause of both $A_3$ and $Y$. So it needs to be adjusted for to get an unbiased estimate of the effect of $A_3$ on $Y$. 
However, since $X_3$ is on the path of $A_2$ to $Y$, by conditioning on $X_3$ 
part of the causal effect of $A_2$ on $Y$ mediated through $X_3$ gets blocked. So methods are needed to both adjust for the 
time-varying confounders while not blocking the causal paths from the earlier treatments. 

\paragraph{Causal target.} In longitudinal settings, different causal 
targets can be defined. For now we focus on the average final potential outcome under a specific treatment 
regime, $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{a}_T)]$, as a fundamental causal target. 
It represents the expected final outcome if all individuals in the population were to follow the treatment 
regime $\bar{a}_T$. This causal estimand is 
of particular interest as it can be used to construct various causal contrasts between different treatment regimes. 
For example, the average causal effect of taking a binary treatment at all time points compared to not 
taking the treatment at all time points can be defined as $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{1}_T) - Y_i(\bar{0}_T)]$.

\paragraph{G-methods.}
There are well-established methods to identify and estimate $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{a}_T)]$, 
when treatment-confounder feedback is present. The g-formula and marginal structural models (MSMs) are 
two members of the Robins' g-methods family that aim directly at this causal target 
\cite{robinsMarginalStructuralModels2000, shinozakiUnderstandingMarginalStructural2020, snowdenImplementationGComputationSimulated2011}.
While g-formula gives the full map $
\bar{a} \mapsto \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_i(\bar{a})\right]$ for all possible treatment regimes, MSMs are 
an imposed model on $\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_i(\bar{a})\right]$. This is particularly 
useful when the number of time points in a study is large and the number of possible treatment regimes grows 
exponentially. If we have a binary treatment at each time point, then there are $2^T$ 
possible treatment regimes. This means for $T=10$ time points, there are already 1024 possible 
treatment regimes. And the problem becomes even more severe when the treatment is not binary. 

\paragraph{Identification under sequential ignorability.}
These methods are relying on the assumptions of sequential ignorability for identification, which 
states that there are no unmeasured confounders at each time point.  More formally, 
sequential ignorability can be expressed as 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:seq-ignorability}
Y_i(\bar{a}_T) \perp A_{it} | \bar{A}_{it-1}, \bar{X}_{it} \quad \text{for all } t \in \{1, \ldots, T\},
\end{equation}

which means that the potential outcome is independent of the treatment at time $t$ given the past treatment and confounder history. 
This assumption is strong and often violated in observational settings. For instance, 
in electronic health record data, the socio-economic status of patients is often 
an important confounder that affects both the treatment decisions and the health 
outcomes, but it is often not measured in the data. For this reason, the 
standard methods may not be valid in many real-world applications,
which motivates the need for methods that can address unmeasured confounding in longitudinal data.

\section{Marginal structural models}
MSMs are models for average potential outcomes under different treatment regimes. 
Although these models can 
be nonparametric \cite{neugebauerNonparametricCausalEffects2007}, they are often specified as a 
parametric model for $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{a}_T)]$:
$$\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{a}_T)] = m(\bar{a}_T; \beta_0)$$
where we use $\beta_0$ to denote the set of true parameters. These models are called marginal 
structural models because they model the mean of the marginal distribution of the potential outcomes, 
rather than the usual conditional mean of the observed outcomes given the treatments and 
confounders, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[Y_i|\bar{A}_T, \bar{X}_T]$, used in association studies. 
A common example of a MSM is 
the linear model $$\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{a}_T)] = m(\bar{a}_T; \beta) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \sum_{t=1}^{T} a_t.$$ 
Unlike the pure association models, the parameters of MSMs have a causal interpretation. In the above linear MSM, 
$\beta_1$ is the expected outcome when no treatment is given at any time point, 
and $\beta_2$ represents the average causal effect of recieving one more treatment at any time point on the expected outcome.

MSMs paramters are often estimated using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) 
\cite{robinsMarginalStructuralModels2000}. 
IPTW creates a pseudo-population in which the treatment assignment is exagenous 
while preserving the causal effect of $\bar{A}$ on $Y$. 
In this pseudo-population, the parameters of the MSM can be estimated using standard regression methods. The 
way IPTW works is by weighting each observation by the inverse of the 
probability of receiving the treatment that they actually received, 
given their past treatment and confounder history. Let $\pi_{it} = \mathbb{P}(A_{it} = 1 | \bar{A}_{it-1}, \bar{X}_{it})$ be 
the true probability of receiving the treatment at time $t$ given the past treatment (propensity score) and confounder history for individual $i$. 
Then the IPTW weight for individual $i$ is defined as 
$w_{i} = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_{it}^{-A_{it}} (1-\pi_{it})^{-(1-A_{it})}$. Using 
these weights, \cite{robinsAssociationCausationMarginal1999} showed that 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ipw-identification}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{\bar A_{iT}=\bar a_T\}W_iY_i\right]=m(\bar a_T,\beta_0).
\end{equation}

In observational studies, the true probabilities $\pi_{it}$ are unknown and need to be 
estimated from the data. This is typically done by fitting a parametric model for the 
treatment assignment mechanism. Let $\pi_{it}(\bar{a}_{t-1}, \bar{x}_{t}; \theta_0) = \mathbb{P}(A_{it} = 1 | \bar{A}_{it-1}, \bar{X}_{it})$ be a 
parametrization of the treatment assignment mechanism, where $\theta_0$ is the true parameter. Then the 
estimated propensity scores are $\hat{\pi}_{it} = \pi_{it}(\bar{A}_{it-1}, \bar{X}_{it}; \hat{\theta})$ where $\hat{\theta}$ is the maximum likelihood estimate of $\theta_0$.
Then the estimated IPTW weights are defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ipw-weights}
\hat{w}_{i} = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \hat{\pi}_{it}^{-A_{it}} (1-\hat{\pi}_{it})^{-(1-A_{it})}, 
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\theta}$ is the maximum likelihood estimate of $\theta_0$. Once the weights are estimated, the IPTW estimator of MSM can be obtained by solving emperical version of the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ipw-estimation}
\mathbb{E}\left\{\hat{w}_i\, h\!\left(\bar{A}_{i,T}\right)\left(Y_i - m\!\left(\bar{A}_{i,T};\beta\right)\right)\right\}=0,
\end{equation}
where $h(\cdot)$ is a researcher-chosen vector valued function of the treatment history with dimensionality of  
$1 \times \dim(\beta)$. If $h(\cdot)$ 
is chosen to be the identity function and the MSM is specified as a linear model, 
then the above estimating equation reduces to a weighted least squares problem.

If the estimated propensity scores be close to zero or one, the IPTW weights can become 
very large, which can lead to unstable estimates. To mitigate this issue, stabilized weights 
are often used, which are defined as 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:stabilized-weights}
\widehat{sw}_i
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{
\hat{\bar{\pi}}_{it}^{A_{it}}\left(1-\hat{\bar{\pi}}_{it}\right)^{1-A_{it}}
}{
\hat{\pi}_{it}^{A_{it}}\left(1-\hat{\pi}_{it}\right)^{1-A_{it}}
}.
\end{equation}
In this equation, ${\hat{\bar{\pi}}}_{it}$ is the estimated 
reduced propensity score, which is the probability of 
receiving the treatment at time $t$ given only the past treatment history:
$
\mathbb{P}(A_{it} = 1 | \bar{A}_{it-1}).
$

The consistency of the IPTW estimator relies on the correct 
specification of the treatment assignment model which is used in the denominator of the stabilized weights. 
The numerator of the stabilized weights is only used to improve the finite sample performance of the estimator, but the misspecification of it does not affect the consistency. 
Moreover, any function of the past treatment history can be used in the numerator as long as it is not a function of the confounders \cite{robinsMarginalStructuralModels2000a}.

\section{Time-invariant unmeasured confounding in longitudinal studies}

\subsection{Fixed-effects approaches}
Using fixed-effect regression to adjust for unmeasured time-invariant confounders is a common approach in 
longitudinal data analysis \cite{imaiWhenShouldWe2019}. The idea is to include an individual-specific intercept 
in the outcome model and estimate the model using the within-individual variation over time. The simplest 
version of this approach when there is no time-varying confounder is to fit a linear model of the form
$$Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta A_{it} + \epsilon_{it},$$
with $\alpha_i$ being the individual-specific intercept that captures the effect of any 
unmeasured time-invariant confounder in an additive form. 

Although these models can adjust for unmeasured time-invariant confounders, They have 
several shortcomings. They are primaarily designed for settings with repeated measures of the outcome, 
and they are not directly applicable to settings with a single terminal outcome. Depending on the 
context, this can be a significant limitation, as many longitudinal studies are interested in the effect of treatments on a final outcome measured at the end of the study. 
More importantly even when there are repeated measures of the outcome, the causal structures in which 
fixed-effect regression can be used to adjust for unmeasured confounding to get valid estimates of causal effects are limited. Specifically, 
they can only be used when there is no treatment-confounder feedback. Another important drawback 
of fixed-effect regression is their inability to target the average potential outcome under a specific treatment regime, which is often of interest in longitudinal studies. Instead, 
they target average sprantaneous effect of the current treatment on the current outcome, i.e. $\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(A_{it}=1) - Y_{it}(A_{it}=0)]$. 
The causal assumptions 
required implicitly by the fixed-effect regression is discussed in detail in \cite{imaiWhenShouldWe2019}. 

In contrast, MSMs can target the average potential outcome under a full treatment regime while allowing for complex
causal paths. However, they cannot adjust for unmeasured confounding. 
The FE-MSM proposed by \cite{blackwellEffectPoliticalAdvertising2024} can be best understood as a 
hybrid of these approaches. TThe novelty of FE-MSM comes from its use of a fixed-effect in 
the propensity score model as opposed to the outcome model. This allows the method 
to adjust for unmeasured time-invariant confounders wihtout relying on repeated outcome measures, 
while still benefiting from the ability of MSMs to adjust for all sorts of causal dynamics 
including treatment-confounder feedback.  
However, this comes at the cost of targeting the average potential outcome under truncated treatment 
interventions instead of the full treatment regimes.

\subsection{Deconfounder theory and sequential deconfounding}
Another approach to address time-invariant unmeasured confounding is based on the 
deconfounder theory proposed by \cite{wangBlessingsMultipleCauses2019}. This method is 
devised for static settings with multiple treatments. The key idea is that if there is 
a a true structural unobserved confounder vector $U$ that affects multiple treatments, then it leaves a 
statistical signature in the observed treatments by inducing conditional independence 
between the treatments: 
$$p(A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}\mid U_i, X_i) =  \prod_{j=1}^{M} p(A_{ij}\mid U_i, X_i)$$
Moreover, if $U_i$ was observed, then the strong ignorability assumption would hold:
$$Y_i(\bar{a}_M) \perp (A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}) | U_i, X_i$$
where $M$ is the number of treatments, $A_{ij}$ is the $j$-th treatment for 
individual $i$, and $X_i$ is the observed covariate vector. 

The deconfounder recepie is then to fit a latent factor model of the assigned 
treatments which renders the same treatment conditional independance structure as the true unobserved confounder $U$ by substituting the latent variable $Z$ instead: 
$$p_{\Theta}(A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}\mid Z_i, X_i) = \prod_{i=1}^N \prod_{j=1}^{M} p(A_{ij}\mid Z_i, X_i,\theta_j)$$
where $\theta_j$ is the treatment-specific parameter. Under the assumptions below and SUTVA,  
this latent variable $Z$ can be used as a substitute for the unobserved confounder $U$ and to retain the ignorability assumption:
$$Y_i(\bar{a}_M) \perp (A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}) | Z_i, X_i$$. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assumption 1: No single-treatment confounder.} There is no 
    unmeasured confounder that affects only one treatment. 
    This assumption is also known as single-cause ignorability assumption. 
    \item \textbf{Assumption 2: Consistency of the substitute confounders.} The latent variable $Z$ is 
    pinpointed almost surely by a single deterministic function of the observed treatments:
    $$ P(Z \mid A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_M) = \delta_{f(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_M)}. $$
    where $\delta_{f(\cdot)}$ denotes a point mass at $f(\cdot)$. This means that the latent variable 
    $Z$ is a deterministic function of the observed treatments and it implies the uniquness of the latent variable \cite{wangClarifyingTheoryDeconfounder2020}. 
    \item \textbf{Assumption 3: Positivity or overlap.} The last $k$ treatments satisfy 
    overlap, $p(A_{i,(M-k+1):M} = a_{i,(M-k+1):M} | Z_i, X_i) > 0$ for all $a_{i,(M-k+1):M}$ in the support of $A_{i,(M-k+1):M}$ and for some $k \ge 1$.
    This assumption is needed for identification of the average potential outcome of subsets of the treatments,
    $\mathbf{E}[Y_i\left(A_{1:M-k}, a_{i,(M-k+1):M}\right)]$. This represents an intervene only on the last 
    $k$ treatments while keeping the remaining treatments at their observed values.
\end{itemize}

and use the estimated latent variable as a substitute for the unobserved confounder.

This means that if we could find a latent variable $Z$ that renders the

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:deconfounder-ignorability}
    Y_i(\bar{a}_M) \perp (A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}) | Z_i, X_i
\end{equation}

In another word,
