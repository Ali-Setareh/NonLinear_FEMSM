\chapter{Background and related work}
\label{chap:literature_review}

Methods for observational longitudinal causal inference with discrete time points can be organized along two main dimensions: 
the structure of the causal process and the causal target of interest. 
The structure of the causal process is typically represented by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) 
that encodes the assumptions about the data-generating process and the causal relationships 
between the variables \cite{pearlCausalityModelsReasoning2009}. The presence or absence of 
treatment-confounder feedback 
and unobserved confounders are important features of the causal structure that can affect the 
choice of the estimation method. The causal target of interest, on the other hand, is driven by the 
research question and scientific context of the study \cite{lundbergWhatYourEstimand2021}. Different methods may be designed to estimate 
different causal targets, such as the average treatment effect at a specific time point, the cumulative 
effect of treatment over time, or the average contrast between two specific treatment regimes. Having these 
two dimensions in mind, in the 
following sections, we will review the relevant literature and concepts on longitudinal causal inference 
and the methods that exist to address confounding.

\section{Conceptual foundations of longitudinal causal inference}
\paragraph{Potential outcomes and treatment regimes}
The causal targets are often formalized in the potential-outcomes framework \cite{rubinEstimatingCausalEffects1974}. 
In this framework, the potential outcome of individual $i$ is defined as $Y_i(a)$, representing the outcome that would be observed if 
the treatment $A$ were set to a specific value $a$. Note the difference between the observed outcome $Y_i$ and the 
potential outcome $Y_i(a)$ which is an unobserved variable, 
as we can only observe the outcome under the treatment that was actually assigned to. Similarly, in the longitudinal setting, we can define the potential outcome at time $t$ for a 
static treatment regime\footnote{The static here is to contrast with a 
dynamic treatment regime, where treatments depend on the values of the confounders \cite{moodieDemystifyingOptimalDynamic2007}. In this thesis we only consider static treatment regimes.} 
$\bar{\mathbf{a}}_t = (a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_t)$ as $Y_{i,t+1}(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_t)$, 
representing the outcome that would be observed if the treatment were set to specific values at each 
time point. In single terminal outcome settings with $T$ time points, it is 
common to drop the time index on the potential outcome and simply 
write $Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T)$ to represent the potential outcome at 
the end of the study under the treatment regime $\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T$. 
Similarly, the 
potential value of confounders at time $t$ can also be defined as $X_{it}(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{t-1})$, 
representing the value of the confounder at time $t$ that would be observed if the treatment 
were set to specific values at each time point up to $t-1$\footnote{Here we follow this indexing convention to emphasize the temporal ordering of the treatments, 
confounders and (possibly repeated) outcome. Confounders at time $t$ are measured after the treatment at time $t-1$ 
and before the treatment at time $t$. Moreover, the potential outcome is indexed at time $t+1$ to reflect 
the fact that the outcome is determined after the treatment while the measured outcome at time $t$ may act 
as a confounder for the treatment at time $t$ and the outcomes at later time points.}.

\paragraph{Treatment-confounder feedback.}
Treatment-confounder feedback occurs when the treatment at time $t$ can affect the confounders at later 
time points. This creates a feedback loop between the treatment and the confounders, 
which can lead to bias if it is not properly adjusted for; (see 
\citeA{hernanCausalInferenceWhat2023booksection} 
for detailed examples.) In Figure \ref{fig:sample_dags} (b), these feedback loops 
are represented by the purple arrows from $A_t$ to subsequent $\mathbf{X}_{t+k}$ where $1 \le k \le T-t$. These structures 
are problematic for standard regression-based methods which estimate 
models such as $\mathbb{E}[Y_i|\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i3},\bar{\mathbf{X}}_{i3}] = \alpha + \sum_{t=1}^{3} \beta_t A_{it} + \sum_{t=1}^{3}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_t^\top \mathbf{X}_{it}$, 
even 
if this outcome regression is correctly specified. To see why, in Figure \ref{fig:sample_dags} (b), 
$\mathbf{X}_3$ is a confounder for the effect of $A_3$ on $Y$ as it is a common cause of both $A_3$ and $Y$. So it needs to be adjusted for to get an unbiased estimate of the effect of $A_3$ on $Y$. 
However, since $\mathbf{X}_3$ is on the path of $A_2$ to $Y$, by conditioning on $\mathbf{X}_3$ 
part of the causal effect of $A_2$ on $Y$ mediated through $\mathbf{X}_3$ gets blocked. So methods are needed to both adjust for the 
time-varying confounders while not blocking the causal paths from the earlier treatments. 

\paragraph{Causal target.} In longitudinal settings, different causal 
targets can be defined. One of the most common causal targets is 
the average of the final potential outcome under a specific treatment 
regime, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T)]$, as a fundamental causal target. 
It represents the expected final outcome if all individuals in the population were to follow the treatment 
regime $\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T$. This causal estimand is 
of particular interest as it can be used to construct various causal contrasts between different treatment regimes. 
For example, the average causal effect of taking a binary treatment at all time points compared to not 
taking the treatment at all can be defined as $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{1}}_T) - Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{0}}_T)]$.

\paragraph{G-methods.}
There are well-established methods to identify and estimate $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T)]$, 
when treatment-confounder feedback is present. The g-formula and marginal structural models (MSMs) are 
two members of the Robins' g-methods family that aim directly at this causal target 
\cite{robinsMarginalStructuralModels2000, shinozakiUnderstandingMarginalStructural2020, snowdenImplementationGComputationSimulated2011}.
While the g-formula gives the full map $
\bar{\mathbf{a}} \mapsto \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}})\right]$ for all possible treatment regimes, MSMs are 
an imposed (restricted) model on $\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}})\right]$. This is particularly 
useful when the number of time points in a study is large since the number of possible treatment regimes grows 
exponentially. To see this, if we have a binary treatment at each time point, then there are $2^T$ 
possible treatment regimes. This means for a study with 10 time points, there are already 1024 possible 
treatment regimes. The problem becomes even more severe when the treatment is not binary. 

\paragraph{Identification under sequential ignorability.}
G-methods rely on the assumption of sequential ignorability for identification, which 
states that there are no unmeasured confounders between the 
treatment and outcome at each time point.  More formally, 
sequential ignorability can be expressed as 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:seq-ignorability}
Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T) \perp A_{it} | \bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,t-1}, \bar{\mathbf{X}}_{it} \quad \text{for all } t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}.
\end{equation}
This equation states that the potential outcome is independent of the treatment at time $t$ given the past treatment and confounder history. 
This assumption is strong and often violated in observational settings. For instance, 
in electronic health record data, the socio-economic status of patients is often 
an important confounder that affects both the treatment decisions and the health 
outcomes, but it is often not measured in the data. For this reason, the 
standard methods may not be valid in many real-world applications,
which motivates the need for methods that can address unmeasured confounding in longitudinal data.

\section{Marginal structural models}
MSMs are models for average potential outcomes under different treatment regimes. 
Although these models can 
be nonparametric \cite{neugebauerNonparametricCausalEffects2007}, they are often specified as a 
parametric model for $\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T)]$:
$$\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T)] = m(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T; \boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$$
where we use $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$ to denote the set of true parameters. These models are called marginal 
structural models because they model the mean of the marginal distribution of the potential outcomes, 
rather than the usual conditional mean of the observed outcomes given the treatments and 
confounders, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[Y_i|\bar{\mathbf{A}}_T, \bar{\mathbf{X}}_T]$, used in association studies. 
A common example of a MSM is 
the linear model $$\mathbb{E}[Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T)] = m(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \theta_1 + \theta_2 \sum_{t=1}^{T} a_t.$$ 
Unlike the pure association models, the parameters of MSMs have a causal interpretation. If this model is 
correctly specified,
$\theta_1$ is the expected outcome when no treatment is given at any time point, 
and $\theta_2$ represents the average causal effect of receiving one more treatment on the expected outcome.

MSM parameters are often estimated using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) 
\cite{robinsMarginalStructuralModels2000}. 
IPTW creates a pseudo-population in which the treatment assignment is exogenous 
while preserving the causal effect of $\bar{\mathbf{A}}$ on $Y$. 
In this pseudo-population, the parameters of the MSM can be estimated using standard regression methods. The 
way IPTW works is by weighting each observation by the inverse of the 
probability of receiving the treatment, 
given their past treatment and confounder history. For binary treatments, 
let $\pi_{it} = \mathbb{P}(A_{it} = 1 | \bar{\mathbf{A}}_{it-1}, \bar{\mathbf{X}}_{it})$ be 
the true probability of receiving the treatment at time $t$ given the past treatment  and confounder history for individual $i$ (the propensity score). 
Then the IPTW weight for individual $i$ is defined as 
$W_{i} = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_{it}^{-A_{it}} (1-\pi_{it})^{-(1-A_{it})}$. Using 
these weights, \citeA{robinsAssociationCausationMarginal1999} showed that 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ipw-identification}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,T}=\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T\}W_iY_i\right]=m(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_T;\boldsymbol{\theta}_0).
\end{equation}

In observational studies, the true probabilities $\pi_{it}$ are unknown and need to be 
estimated from the data. This is typically done by fitting a parametric model for the 
treatment assignment mechanism. Let $\pi_{it}(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{t-1}, \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}_0) = \mathbb{P}(A_{it} = 1 | \bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,t-1} = \bar{\mathbf{a}}_{t-1}, \bar{\mathbf{X}}_{it} = \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t})$ be a 
parametrization of the treatment assignment mechanism, with $\boldsymbol{\beta}_0$ denoting the true parameter. The 
estimated propensity scores are then given by $\hat{\pi}_{it} = \pi_{it}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,t-1}, \bar{\mathbf{X}}_{it}; \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})$ where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is the maximum likelihood estimate of $\boldsymbol{\beta}_0$.
The estimated IPTW weights are
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ipw-weights}
\widehat{W}_{i} = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \hat{\pi}_{it}^{-A_{it}} (1-\hat{\pi}_{it})^{-(1-A_{it})}.
\end{equation}
Once the weights are estimated, the IPTW estimator of MSM can be obtained by solving the empirical version of the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ipw-estimation}
\mathbb{E}\left\{\widehat{W}_i\, \mathbf{h}\!\left(\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,T}\right)\left(Y_i - m\!\left(\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,T};\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right)\right\}=0,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}(\cdot)$ is a researcher-chosen vector-valued function of the treatment history with dimensionality of  
$1 \times \dim(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. If $\mathbf{h}(\cdot)$ 
is chosen to be the identity function and the MSM is specified as a linear model, 
then the above estimating equation reduces to a weighted least squares problem.

The IPTW weights can become 
very large when the estimated propensity scores are close to zero or one,
which may lead to unstable estimates. To mitigate this issue, stabilized weights 
are often used.  They are defined as 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:stabilized-weights}
\widehat{SW}_i
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{
\hat{\bar{\pi}}_{it}^{A_{it}}\left(1-\hat{\bar{\pi}}_{it}\right)^{1-A_{it}}
}{
\hat{\pi}_{it}^{A_{it}}\left(1-\hat{\pi}_{it}\right)^{1-A_{it}}
}.
\end{equation}
In the numerator, ${\hat{\bar{\pi}}}_{it}$ is the estimated 
reduced propensity score, that is, the probability of 
receiving the treatment at time $t$ given only the past treatment history, 
\[
\bar{\pi}_{it} = \mathbb{P}(A_{it} = 1 | \bar{\mathbf{A}}_{i,t-1}).
\]

The consistency of the IPTW estimator only relies on the correct 
specification of the treatment assignment model appearing in the denominator of the stabilized weights. 
The numerator serves only to improve the finite-sample performance of the estimator, but the misspecification 
of it does not affect the consistency. 
Moreover, any function of the past treatment history can be included in the numerator as long as it is not a function 
of the confounders \cite{robinsMarginalStructuralModels2000a}.

\section{Time-invariant unmeasured confounding in longitudinal studies}

\subsection{Fixed-effects approaches}
Using fixed-effect regression to adjust for unmeasured time-invariant confounders is a common approach in 
longitudinal data analysis \cite{imaiWhenShouldWe2019}. The idea is to include an individual-specific intercept 
in the outcome model and estimate the model using the within-individual variation over time. The simplest 
version of this approach when there is no time-varying confounder is to fit a linear model of the form
$$Y_{i,t+1} = \alpha_i + \beta A_{it} + \epsilon_{it},$$
with $\alpha_i$ being the individual-specific intercept that captures the effect of any 
unmeasured time-invariant confounder in an additive form. 

Although these models can adjust for unmeasured time-invariant confounders, they have 
several shortcomings. They are primarily designed for settings with repeated measured outcomes, 
and are not directly applicable to designs with a single terminal outcome. Depending on the 
context, this can be a significant limitation, as many longitudinal studies are 
interested in the effect of treatments on a final outcome measured at the end of the study. 
More importantly, even when there are repeated measures of the outcome, the causal structures in which 
fixed-effects regression can be used to adjust for unmeasured confounding to get valid estimates of causal effects are limited. Specifically, 
they can only be used when there is no treatment-confounder feedback. Another important drawback 
of fixed-effects regression is its inability to target the average 
potential outcome under specific treatment regimes, which is often of 
interest in longitudinal studies. Instead, 
they target the average spontaneous effect of the current treatment 
on the current outcome, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t+1}(a_{it}=1) - Y_{i,t+1}(a_{it}=0)]$. 
The assumptions 
required implicitly by the fixed-effects regression to be causally interpretable are discussed in 
detail by \citeA{imaiWhenShouldWe2019}. 

In contrast, MSMs can target the average potential outcome under a full treatment regime while allowing for complex
causal paths. However, they cannot adjust for unmeasured confounding. 
The FE-MSM proposed by \citeA{blackwellEffectPoliticalAdvertising2024} can be best understood as a 
hybrid of these approaches. The novelty of FE-MSM comes from its use of a fixed-effect in 
the propensity score model as opposed to the outcome model. This allows the method 
to adjust for unmeasured time-invariant confounders without relying on repeated outcome measures, 
while still benefiting from the ability of MSMs to adjust for a wide range of causal dynamics 
including treatment-confounder feedback.  
However, this comes at the cost of targeting the average potential outcome under truncated treatment 
interventions instead of the full treatment regimes.

\subsection{Deconfounder theory and sequential deconfounding}
\subsubsection{Multi-treatment deconfounder}
Another approach to address time-invariant unmeasured confounding is based on the 
deconfounder theory proposed by \cite{wangBlessingsMultipleCauses2019}. This method is originally 
devised for static settings with multiple treatments. The key idea is that if there is 
a true structural unobserved confounder vector $\mathbf{U}_i$ that affects multiple treatments, then it leaves a 
statistical signature in the observed treatments by inducing conditional independence 
between the treatments: 
$$\mathbb{P}(A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}\mid \mathbf{U}_i, \mathbf{X}_i) =  \prod_{j=1}^{M} \mathbb{P}(A_{ij}\mid \mathbf{U}_i, \mathbf{X}_i)$$
Moreover, if $\mathbf{U}_i$ was observed, then the strong ignorability assumption would hold:
$$Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_M) \perp (A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}) | \mathbf{U}_i, \mathbf{X}_i$$
where $M$ is the number of treatments, $A_{ij}$ is the $j$-th treatment for 
individual $i$, and $\mathbf{X}_i$ is the observed covariate vector. 

The deconfounder recipe is then to fit a latent factor model of the assigned 
treatments which renders the same treatment conditional independence structure as the true unobserved confounder $\mathbf{U}_i$ 
by substituting the latent variable $\mathbf{Z}_i$ instead: 
$$\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}(A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}\mid \mathbf{Z}_i, \mathbf{X}_i) = \prod_{j=1}^{M} \mathbb{P}(A_{ij}\mid \mathbf{Z}_i, \mathbf{X}_i,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_j)$$
where $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_j$ is the treatment-specific parameter. Under the assumptions below and SUTVA,  
this latent variable $\mathbf{Z}_i$ can be used as a substitute for the unobserved confounder $\mathbf{U}_i$ 
and to retain the ignorability assumption:
$$Y_i(\bar{\mathbf{a}}_M) \perp (A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}) | \mathbf{Z}_i, \mathbf{X}_i.$$
Note that the substitute confounder $\mathbf{Z}_i$ is not necessarily the same as the true 
unobserved confounder $\mathbf{U}_i$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assumption 1: No single-treatment confounder.} There is no 
    unmeasured confounder that affects only one treatment. Or in other words, 
    all unmeasured confounders are multi-treatment confounders that affect at least two treatments.
    This assumption is also known as single-cause ignorability assumption. 
    \item \textbf{Assumption 2: Consistency of the substitute confounders.} The latent variable $\mathbf{Z}_i$ is 
    pinpointed almost surely by a single deterministic function of the observed treatments:
    $$ \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{Z}_i \mid A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM}) = \delta_{\mathbf{f}   (A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM})}. $$
    Here, $\delta_{\mathbf{f}(\cdot)}$ denotes a point mass at $\mathbf{f}(\cdot)$. This means that the latent variable 
    $\mathbf{Z}_i$ is a deterministic function of the observed treatments, and it implies the 
    uniqueness of the latent variable \cite{wangClarifyingTheoryDeconfounder2020}. 
    \item \textbf{Assumption 3: Positivity or overlap.} The last $k$ treatments satisfy 
    overlap, $\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A}_{i,(M-k+1):M} = \mathbf{a}_{i,(M-k+1):M} | \mathbf{Z}_i, \mathbf{X}_i) > 0$ for all $\mathbf{a}_{i,(M-k+1):M}$ in the support of $\mathbf{A}_{i,(M-k+1):M}$ and for some $k \ge 1$.
    This assumption is needed for identification of the average truncated potential outcomes,
    $\mathbb{E}[Y_i\left(\mathbf{A}_{1:M-k}, \mathbf{a}_{i,(M-k+1):M}\right)]$, where only the last $k$ treatments 
    are intervened on while the remaining treatments are kept at their observed values.  
   
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Debates on multi-treatment deconfounder}

Since the introduction of the multi-treatment deconfounder, it has initiated several extensive 
debates in the literature about the validity of the assumptions. Most of the criticisms are about the 
consistency assumption and its tension with the positivity assumption. 
\citeA{damourMultiCauseApproachesCausal2019} notes that factorization existence does not guarantee 
the uniqueness of the latent variable and derives an equivalence class of 
latent variables that can all render the same treatment conditional independence structure,
while 
resulting in different causal effects ranging from negative to positive. This counterexample shows 
that consistency assumption should be understood as a property of the underlying 
data-generating process and not as a consequence of a particular factor model. Hence, 
it cannot be verified by the observed data. 

Another point raised by \citeA{damourMultiCauseApproachesCausal2019} 
is that, if the consistency assumption holds, %the latent variables are a 
%deterministic function of the observed treatments. Consequently, 
there is no variability 
in the treatments given the latent variable which violates the positivity assumption. Hence, 
consistency and positivity assumptions cannot hold simultaneously.  
However, if the causal target of interest is the average potential 
outcome under interventions on subsets of the treatments, then the positivity assumption can still 
hold 
for that subset while the consistency assumption holds for the full set of treatments as 
stated by Theorem 6 in \citeA{wangBlessingsMultipleCauses2019}. 

The other criticism is concerning whether the deconfounder's assumptions are truly weaker than the standard 
ignorability assumption. \citeA{grimmerNaiveRegressionRequires2023} argues that in most settings where
the deconfounder assumptions hold, the naive regression adjustment with only the 
observed covariates also results in unbiased estimates of the causal effects. Moreover, through 
extensive simulation studies, they showed 
that both the deconfounder method and the naive regression adjustment perform equally poorly in the presence 
of unmeasured confounding. 

Furthermore, \citeA{ogburnCommentBlessingsMultiple2019} note that if $\mathbf{Z}_i$ is 
a deterministic function of the observed treatments, i.e., $\mathbf{Z}_i = \mathbf{f}(A_{i1}, A_{i2}, \ldots, A_{iM})$, 
then $\mathbf{Z}_i$ is a post-treatment variable and conditioning on it may cause biased estimates of 
the causal effects. 

Some of these criticisms were addressed by \citeA{wangBlessingsMultipleCauses2019a} 
and \citeA{wangClarifyingTheoryDeconfounder2020}. However, the ambiguity surrounding the consistency 
assumption and the constraints it imposes on the substitute confounder, still remains 
a major concern for the deconfounder method to be widely applicable. 

\subsubsection{Longitudinal extensions of the deconfounder}
The deconfounder method has been extended to longitudinal settings 
by \citeA{bicaTimeSeriesDeconfounder2020} and \citeA{hattSequentialDeconfoundingCausal2024}. 
They both rely on the 
same key idea as the original deconfounder method, which is to use a 
latent variable to capture the statistical signature of the unobserved 
confounders in the observed treatments. Their main differences lie in the causal structures they are designed for 
and the causal targets they can identify.
While the method of \citeA{bicaTimeSeriesDeconfounder2020} is designed
for settings with multiple treatments 
at each time point to adjust for time-varying unmeasured confounders using dynamic 
latent variables, 
\citeA{hattSequentialDeconfoundingCausal2024} is designed for a common setting 
with a single treatment at each time point in the presence of constant 
unmeasured confounding, and it estimates a constant latent variable using 
the treatment history. Moreover, they both require the consistency assumption which 
is the most controversial assumption and its implementations will 
be discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}. A graphical comparison of 
the sequential deconfounder and the multi-treatment deconfounder is presented in Figure 
\ref{fig:deconfounder-comparison}.

\section{Research gap and thesis contribution}

The literature reviewed in this chapter shows that longitudinal causal inference methods differ not only in the causal structures 
they are designed to handle, but also in the causal targets they aim to identify. Standard g-methods such as marginal structural 
models provide a principled way to estimate average potential outcomes under treatment regimes in the presence of treatment-confounder 
feedback. However, their validity depends on sequential ignorability, an assumption that may be implausible in many observational studies 
when important baseline characteristics are unmeasured. Fixed-effect approaches and deconfounder-based methods offer two different strategies 
for addressing time-invariant unmeasured confounding, but they do so under different assumptions and with different implications for the causal 
estimands that can be identified.

The present thesis is positioned at this intersection. It compares two recent approaches for estimating causal effects of time-varying treatments 
in the presence of time-invariant unmeasured confounding: the fixed-effect marginal structural model and the sequential Gaussian process latent variable model. 
Rather than treating these methods as unrelated contributions from different research traditions, this thesis presents them within a common notation and highlights 
their similarities and differences in terms of their assumptions, estimation procedures, and causal targets. In doing so, it clarifies how the fixed-effect and 
latent-variable perspectives each attempt to recover information about unobserved heterogeneity from longitudinal treatment histories. 
These contributions aim to make the relationship between the two methods more transparent 
and to support future methodological work on longitudinal causal inference with time-invariant unmeasured confounding.
